PART A — Chapter 8: Factorising
Hoofstuk 8 · Eksamenfokus bl164

A1. The Golden Order — do these 4 checks, in this order, every time

Factorising is not guessing. It is a decision tree. Ask the questions in order and stop when the expression is fully broken down.

1. Common factor?Take out the HCF of every term — numbers and letters. Always do this first, always.
2. Two terms?Difference of two squares:
a² − b² = (a−b)(a+b)
3. Three terms?Trinomial: find two numbers that × to give the last term and + to give the middle.
4. Four terms?Group in pairs, take out a common factor from each pair, then take out the common bracket.
"Factorise fully" means keep going

After you factorise once, look at each bracket again and run the checklist on it. Marks are lost every year for stopping at 8(x⁴−1) when x⁴−1 still factorises. If a bracket has 2 terms and both are perfect squares, it factorises again.

A2. Rule 1 — Common factor (gemeenskaplike faktor)

Find the highest common factor of all terms and write it outside a bracket. Divide each original term by the HCF to get what goes inside.

Rule

ka + kb = k(a + b)  — for the letters, take the lowest power that appears in every term.

Worked — (d)(1)  7x + 14

HCF of 7 and 14 is 7. There is no x in the second term, so no letter comes out. 7x + 14 = 7(x + 2)   — check: 7 × x = 7x ✓, 7 × 2 = 14 ✓ = 7(x + 2)

Worked — (d)(7)  4a² + 3a

Numbers: HCF of 4 and 3 is 1. Letters: a² and a → lowest power is a. So the HCF is a.   4a² ÷ a = 4a  and  3a ÷ a = 3. = a(4a + 3)
Watch out

Don't write a(4a + 3a). You divide each term by the HCF — you do not just copy it. Multiply back out to check, every time.

A3. Rule 2 — Difference of two squares (verskil van twee kwadrate)

Rule

a² − b² = (a − b)(a + b)

Three conditions, all must be true: (i) exactly two terms, (ii) a minus between them, (iii) both terms are perfect squares.

a² + b² does NOT factorise. A sum of two squares is prime. This is a favourite trap question.

Square roots you must know: √(4m²) = 2m, √(9x²) = 3x, √(25) = 5, √(x⁸) = x⁴ (halve the power), √(16y⁶) = 4y³.

Worked — (d)(10)  4m² − 1

Two terms, minus, both perfect squares: √(4m²) = 2m, √1 = 1. = (2m − 1)(2m + 1)

Worked — (d)(21)  8x⁸ − 8  (the "factorise fully" trap)

Step 1 — common factor first: HCF is 8.   = 8(x⁸ − 1) Step 2 — the bracket has 2 terms, minus, both squares: = 8(x⁴ − 1)(x⁴ + 1) Step 3 — look again! x⁴ − 1 is still a difference of squares: = 8(x² − 1)(x² + 1)(x⁴ + 1) Step 4 — look again! x² − 1 is still a difference of squares: = 8(x − 1)(x + 1)(x² + 1)(x⁴ + 1) Stop here: x² + 1 and x⁴ + 1 are SUMS of squares — they do not factorise.

A4. Rule 3 — Trinomials x² + bx + c (drieterme)

Method

Find two numbers that multiply to give c (the last number) and add to give b (the middle number). Then x² + bx + c = (x + first)(x + second).

The sign table — learn this, it removes all the guessing

Last sign (c)Middle sign (b)Signs in the bracketsWhat the two numbers doExample
++( + )( + ) — both plusADD to b, multiply to cx²+12x+27 = (x+3)(x+9)
+−( − )( − ) — both minusADD to b, multiply to ck²−12k+27 = (k−3)(k−9)
−+( + )( − ) — one of eachSUBTRACT to b; the bigger number is +a²+6a−27 = (a+9)(a−3)
−−( + )( − ) — one of eachSUBTRACT to b; the bigger number is −y²−6y−27 = (y−9)(y+3)
Shortcut to remember it

Last sign PLUS → both brackets get the middle sign, and the numbers add up to the middle number.
Last sign MINUS → the brackets get one plus and one minus, the numbers have a difference equal to the middle number, and the bigger number takes the middle sign.

Factor pairs of 27 (used in all four questions below): 1 × 27 and 3 × 9. Since we need 12 or 6 from them: 3 + 9 = 12 and 9 − 3 = 6.

(d)(22)  x² + 12x + 27

Last +, middle + → both brackets +. Need: × = 27, + = 12 → 3 and 9. = (x + 3)(x + 9)

(d)(24)  a² + 6a − 27

Last − → one +, one −. Need: × = 27, difference = 6 → 9 and 3. Middle is +, so the bigger (9) is +. = (a + 9)(a − 3)

(d)(23)  k² − 12k + 27

Last +, middle − → both brackets −. Need: × = 27, + = 12 → 3 and 9. = (k − 3)(k − 9)

(d)(25)  y² − 6y − 27

Last − → one +, one −. Need: × = 27, difference = 6 → 9 and 3. Middle is −, so the bigger (9) is −. = (y − 9)(y + 3)
Always check by multiplying back

FOIL (y − 9)(y + 3) = y² + 3y − 9y − 27 = y² − 6y − 27 ✓. This takes 5 seconds and catches every sign error.

A5. Rule 4 — Common bracket & grouping (four terms)

Rule 4a — a bracket can be a common factor

If the same bracket appears in every term, take the whole bracket out:
4x(a+b) + 3(a+b) = (a+b)(4x + 3)

Worked — (e)(1)  4x(a + b) + 3(a + b)

The bracket (a + b) is in both terms → it is the common factor. What is left over: 4x from the first term, +3 from the second. = (a + b)(4x + 3)
Rule 4b — the sign-flip trick (VERY important)

(1 − x) = −(x − 1)    (−4 − x) = −(x + 4)    (b − a) = −(a − b)

If two brackets are the same but backwards, take a −1 out of one of them to make them match. Then you can factorise.

Worked — (e)(4)  x²(x − 1) + 4(1 − x)

The brackets are backwards: (x − 1) vs (1 − x). Flip the second: 4(1 − x) = −4(x − 1) = x²(x − 1) − 4(x − 1)  — now the brackets match Take out (x − 1):  = (x − 1)(x² − 4) Factorise fully! x² − 4 is a difference of two squares. = (x − 1)(x − 2)(x + 2)

Worked — Page 2, (5)  x²(x + 4) + 9(−4 − x)

(−4 − x) = −(4 + x) = −(x + 4)  — take out −1, then reorder = x²(x + 4) − 9(x + 4) = (x + 4)(x² − 9)  — difference of squares in the bracket = (x + 4)(x − 3)(x + 3)
Rule 4c — grouping four terms in pairs

Step 1: split into two pairs. Step 2: take a common factor out of each pair. Step 3: the two brackets left must be identical — take that bracket out.

If the brackets are not identical, either re-pair the terms differently, or take out a negative from the second pair.

Worked — (e)(7)  ax + 3a + bx + 3b

Pair them: (ax + 3a) + (bx + 3b) Common factor of pair 1 is a; of pair 2 is b: = a(x + 3) + b(x + 3)  — the brackets match ✓ Take out (x + 3): = (x + 3)(a + b)
If the pairs don't match

Try the other pairing: (ax + bx) + (3a + 3b) = x(a + b) + 3(a + b) = (a + b)(x + 3) — the same answer. Order of brackets doesn't matter.

PART B — Simplifying algebraic fractions
Vereenvoudig

B1. The method — three steps, never anything else

Method
  1. Factorise the top (numerator) completely.
  2. Factorise the bottom (denominator) completely.
  3. Cancel whole brackets / whole factors that appear top and bottom.
The golden rule of cancelling

You may only cancel factors (things being multiplied). You may never cancel terms (things being added or subtracted).

x + 3x — you cannot cancel the x's.    (x+3)(x−1)(x−1) = x + 3 — you can cancel the bracket. ✓

Worked — (i)(2)  x³y − 4xy²x²y²

Top: common factor of x³y and 4xy² is xy → xy(x² − 4y) Bottom: x²y² = xy · xy xy(x² − 4y)x²y² → cancel one x and one y = x² − 4yxy Do NOT cancel further — x² − 4y is a subtraction, not a product.

Worked — (i)(3)  x² − 9x² − 3x

Top: difference of squares → (x − 3)(x + 3) Bottom: common factor x → x(x − 3) (x − 3)(x + 3)x(x − 3) → cancel (x − 3) = x + 3x

Worked — (i)(4)  x² − 14x + 24x² − 11x − 12

Top: × = 24, + = 14, both signs − → 2 and 12 → (x − 2)(x − 12) Bottom: last is − → one + one −; × = 12, difference = 11 → 12 and 1; middle is − so bigger is − → (x − 12)(x + 1) (x − 2)(x − 12)(x − 12)(x + 1) → cancel (x − 12) = x − 2x + 1

Worked — (i)(5)  x² − 14xx² − 16x + 28

Top: common factor x → x(x − 14) Bottom: × = 28, + = 16, both − → 2 and 14 → (x − 14)(x − 2) x(x − 14)(x − 14)(x − 2) → cancel (x − 14) = xx − 2
Exam habit

If the top and bottom don't share a bracket after factorising, you have probably made a sign mistake in one of the trinomials — go back and check. These questions are designed to cancel.

C1. The Theorem of Pythagoras

The theorem

In a right-angled triangle, the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides:

a² + b² = c²

where c is the hypotenuse — the side opposite the right angle, and always the longest side.

Which operation? Add or subtract?
What you are looking forWhat you doFormula
The hypotenuse (longest side, opposite 90°)ADD the two squares, then √c = √(a² + b²)
A shorter side (a leg)SUBTRACT the smaller square from the hypotenuse squared, then √a = √(c² − b²)
The #1 Pythagoras mistake

Adding when you should subtract. Before you calculate, ask: is the side I want the longest side of that triangle? If yes → add. If no → subtract. A shorter side can never come out bigger than the hypotenuse — if it does, you added by mistake.

Surd form vs decimals (wortelvorm)

  • "Leave your answer in surd form" / "gee jou antwoord in wortelvorm" → keep the √ sign, do not round. Simplify it if you can: √98 = √(49 × 2) = 7√2.
  • "Correct to two decimal places" → use the calculator and round: √87 = 9.327... ≈ 9.33.
  • To simplify a surd, split off the largest perfect square factor: √50 = √25·√2 = 5√2, √72 = √36·√2 = 6√2, √12 = 2√3, √98 = 7√2.

Perfect squares & Pythagorean triples worth memorising

Squares: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144, 169, 196, 225, 256, 289, 324, 361, 400.

Triples (and their multiples) — spotting these saves time: 3,4,5 · 5,12,13 · 8,15,17 · 7,24,25 · 20,21,29.
Multiples count too: (5,12,13) × 4 = (20,48,52) and (20,21,29) × 4 = (80,84,116) — both appear on this worksheet!

C2. Two-triangle problems — Exercise 13.1 (d)(1)

The rule: when two right-angled triangles share a side, solve the triangle where you know two sides first, then use that answer in the second triangle.

Right angles at B (in △ABC) and at C (in △ADC). AB = 12, BC = 5, AD = 16.

Find x (= AC) — use △ABC

Right angle is at B, so AC is the hypotenuse → ADD. AC² = AB² + BC² x² = 12² + 5² = 144 + 25 = 169 x = √169   x = 13  (the 5–12–13 triple)

Find y (= DC) — use △ADC

Right angle is at C, so AD (16) is the hypotenuse, and DC is a shorter side → SUBTRACT. AD² = DC² + AC²  →  16² = y² + 13² y² = 256 − 169 = 87 y = √87   y ≈ 9.33  (2 d.p.; in surd form √87 — 87 = 3 × 29 has no square factor, so it cannot simplify)
Notice

You could not find y first — △ADC only had one known side until x gave you the second. Always look for the triangle with two known sides.

C3. The CONVERSE of Pythagoras — proving a right angle

Converse

If the square on the longest side equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides, then the triangle is right-angled, and the right angle is opposite the longest side.

How to lay out a "show that" answer (this is how marks are awarded)
  1. Identify the longest side.
  2. Work out LHS = (longest side)².
  3. Work out RHS = (short side)² + (other short side)².
  4. Write LHS = RHS.
  5. Conclude: ∴ △ABC is right-angled (converse of Pythagoras), and ∴ the right angle is at ___ — the vertex opposite the longest side.

13.2 (a)(1) — In △ABC: AB = 20, BC = 48, AC = 52

Longest side = AC = 52. LHS: AC² = 52² = 2704 RHS: AB² + BC² = 20² + 48² = 400 + 2304 = 2704 ∴ AC² = AB² + BC² ∴ △ABC is right-angled, with the right angle at B  — B is opposite AC (converse of Pythagoras) Sketch: a triangle with a square-corner mark at B, 20 and 48 on the sides meeting at B, 52 across from B.

13.2 (a)(2) — In △ABC: c = 17, b = 8, a = 15

Small letters name the side opposite the matching capital: a is opposite A, b opposite B, c opposite C. Longest side = c = 17. LHS: c² = 17² = 289 RHS: a² + b² = 15² + 8² = 225 + 64 = 289 ∴ c² = a² + b² ∴ △ABC is right-angled, with the right angle at C  — C is opposite side c, the longest side (the 8–15–17 triple)

C4. Acute-angled or obtuse-angled? (skerphoekig of stomphoekig)

The test — compare (longest side)² with the sum of the other two squares
If…Then the triangle is…Where is the special angle?
c² = a² + b²Right-angled (reghoekig)90° opposite the longest side
c² < a² + b²Acute-angled (skerphoekig) — all angles < 90°—
c² > a² + b²Obtuse-angled (stomphoekig)The obtuse angle is opposite the longest side
How to remember

The bigger the longest side is compared to the other two, the more it is "stretched open" — so bigger c² → bigger (obtuse) angle. Small c² → the angle closes up → acute.

13.2 (b)(1) — In △ABC: AB = 24, BC = 14, AC = 27

Longest side = AC = 27.   It is opposite B. AC² = 27² = 729 AB² + BC² = 24² + 14² = 576 + 196 = 772 729 < 772, so AC² < AB² + BC² ∴ △ABC is acute-angled  (all three angles are less than 90°, so there is no obtuse angle to name)

13.2 (b)(2) — In △DEF: d = 8, e = 18, f = 13

Longest side = e = 18.   Side e is opposite E. e² = 18² = 324 d² + f² = 8² + 13² = 64 + 169 = 233 324 > 233, so e² > d² + f² ∴ △DEF is obtuse-angled, and the obtuse angle is Ê  (the angle at E, opposite the longest side)

C5. Exam Focus p239 — squares and rectangles

Facts you must know about squares and rectangles
  • All four angles are 90°, so a diagonal always creates a right-angled triangle — that is your Pythagoras triangle.
  • In a square, all four sides are equal. Diagonal of a square of side s is s√2.
  • In a rectangle, opposite sides are equal.
  • The diagonals of a rectangle are equal AND they bisect each other (cut each other in half). So if they meet at E: AE = EC = BE = ED = ½ × diagonal.

(b) ABCD is a square with side 7 cm. Calculate BD, in surd form.

BD is a diagonal → it makes right-angled △ABD (right angle at A). All sides of a square are equal, so AB = AD = 7 cm. BD is opposite the right angle → BD is the hypotenuse → ADD. BD² = AB² + AD² = 7² + 7² = 49 + 49 = 98 BD = √98 = √(49 × 2) = √49 × √2 BD = 7√2 cm  — surd form: do NOT write 9.90

(c) ABCD is a rectangle, AD = 84 and AB = 80. Calculate AE.

E is where the diagonals cross, so AE = ½ AC. First find the whole diagonal AC. △ABC has a right angle at B, with AB = 80 and BC = AD = 84 (opposite sides of a rectangle are equal). AC is the hypotenuse → ADD: AC² = 80² + 84² = 6400 + 7056 = 13456 AC = √13456 = 116  (it's the 20–21–29 triple × 4) The diagonals of a rectangle bisect each other: AE = ½ × 116   AE = 58
Don't stop early

The question asks for AE, not AC. Finding 116 and writing that down loses the last mark. Underline what the question actually asks for before you start.

Final 60 seconds before the test

Factorising: Common factor → 2 terms (difference of squares) → 3 terms (trinomial + sign table) → 4 terms (grouping). Then check every bracket again.

Fractions: Factorise top, factorise bottom, cancel brackets only.

Pythagoras: a² + b² = c², c = longest = opposite 90°. Hypotenuse → ADD. Shorter side → SUBTRACT. Then √.

Converse: longest² = sum of other two squares → right-angled, 90° opposite the longest side. Less than → acute. Greater than → obtuse (obtuse angle opposite the longest side).

Show every step. Method marks are real marks. Good luck! 🍀
